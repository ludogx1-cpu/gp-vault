Looking after your dog

The everyday basics for a happy, healthy dog, written by dog lovers in the UK. Every dog is different, so treat this as a starting point and ask your vet about anything specific to yours.

🐾 A simple daily routine

  • Fresh water available all day, in a clean bowl.
  • Meals at regular times. Most adult dogs do well on two meals a day; puppies need three or four.
  • Exercise every day, matched to your dog's age, breed and health.
  • Toilet breaks first thing, after meals, after naps and before bed.
  • Something for the brain: a short training session, a sniff walk or a puzzle feeder.
  • A quick once-over: eyes, ears, paws and coat. You'll spot changes early.
  • A quiet, comfy bed they can go to when they want to rest.

🍽️ Feeding

  • Choose a "complete" food (it says so on the pack) made for your dog's life stage: puppy, adult or senior. "Complementary" foods and treats aren't a full diet on their own.
  • Use the pack's feeding guide as a starting point, then adjust to keep your dog in good shape: you should be able to feel the ribs easily and see a waist from above.
  • Treats, chews and table scraps count. Keep treats to roughly a tenth of daily food and take them out of the meal allowance.
  • Change foods gradually over 7–10 days, mixing more of the new food in each day, to avoid an upset tummy.
  • Avoid hard exercise for about an hour either side of a big meal, especially for large, deep-chested breeds.

🦮 Walks & play

  • Most healthy adult dogs need at least one good walk a day, and many working and terrier breeds need far more. Puppies, seniors and flat-faced breeds need shorter, gentler outings.
  • Let them sniff. Sniffing is how dogs explore, and a slow "sniffari" tires the brain as much as a fast walk tires the legs.
  • Only let your dog off the lead where it's allowed and safe, and once their recall is reliable. Always on lead near roads and livestock.
  • Bag it and bin it: always pick up after your dog. Any bin will do if there isn't a dog bin.
  • Mix it up with fetch, tug, hide-and-seek and scent games at home on rainy days.

✂️ Grooming

  • Brush to suit the coat: short coats about once a week, long or double coats (like a Shiba's) several times a week, and daily when they're shedding.
  • Nails: if you can hear them clicking on a hard floor, they probably need a trim. Ask your vet or groomer to show you how.
  • Teeth: brushing with a dog toothpaste (never human toothpaste) is the best way to prevent dental disease. Start slowly with lots of praise.
  • Ears: check weekly for redness, smell or a lot of wax.
  • Baths only when needed, with a dog shampoo. Too often can dry the skin.

💉 Health checks & prevention

Vaccinations

Puppies need a course of vaccinations, then adult dogs need regular boosters. Your vet will advise on the right schedule for your dog and area. Kennels and dog sitters usually ask to see an up-to-date vaccination record.

Fleas, ticks and worms

Use a vet-recommended flea and worming treatment on a regular schedule, and check for ticks after walks in long grass or woodland. Some treatments are toxic to cats, so check before using them in a home with cats.

Yearly check-up

A yearly health check lets your vet catch problems early. Older dogs often benefit from a check every six months.

Neutering and insurance

Talk to your vet about whether and when to neuter. Pet insurance can help with unexpected bills; read what's covered before you choose.

⚠️ Foods and things that are toxic to dogs

Keep these out of reach. If your dog eats any of them, call your vet straight away, even if they seem fine.

  • Chocolate (dark and cooking chocolate are the most dangerous)
  • Grapes, raisins, sultanas and currants, including in cakes, hot cross buns and mince pies
  • Xylitol, a sweetener in some sugar-free gum, sweets, peanut butters and toothpastes
  • Onions, garlic, leeks and chives, raw or cooked
  • Macadamia nuts
  • Alcohol and caffeine
  • Cooked bones, which can splinter
  • Human medicines such as ibuprofen and paracetamol, unless your vet prescribes them
  • Slug pellets, rat poison and antifreeze
  • Lilies are very dangerous to cats; many garden plants, bulbs and conkers can also upset dogs

☀️❄️ Hot and cold weather

In the heat

  • Never leave a dog in a car on a warm day, even with the windows open. Cars heat up fast.
  • Walk early in the morning or late in the evening. Test the pavement with the back of your hand: if it's too hot for you to hold for 5 seconds, it's too hot for paws.
  • Always carry water and give plenty of shade.
  • Signs of heatstroke include heavy panting, drooling, weakness, vomiting or collapse. Move your dog somewhere cool, wet them with cool (not ice-cold) water and call a vet immediately.

In the cold

  • Short-coated, small and older dogs may appreciate a coat.
  • Wipe paws after walks on gritted roads; grit and salt can irritate them.
  • Keep antifreeze well away; it tastes sweet and is very poisonous.
  • Use reflective gear or a light-up collar on dark winter evenings.

🚑 When to call the vet straight away

  • Trouble breathing, collapse or a seizure
  • Swollen, hard tummy or retching without bringing anything up (this can be an emergency in large breeds)
  • Eating something toxic or a foreign object
  • Repeated vomiting or diarrhoea, or any with blood
  • Not passing urine, or straining
  • A serious injury, heavy bleeding or a suspected broken bone
  • Signs of heatstroke

Save your vet's out-of-hours number in your phone today. If you're ever unsure, ring and ask; that's what they're there for.

📋 UK rules every dog owner should know

  • Microchip: dogs in the UK must be microchipped by 8 weeks old. Keep your details on the chip database up to date when you move or change your number.
  • ID tag: in public, your dog must wear a collar and tag with your name and address (a phone number is a good idea too).
  • Under control: it's against the law to let a dog be dangerously out of control anywhere, including at home.
  • Clearing up: many councils fine owners who don't pick up after their dog.
  • Around livestock: keep dogs on a lead near sheep, cows and horses.

Rules differ slightly between England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, and can change. Check GOV.UK or your local council for the details where you live.
