How to turn an AI video into a game sprite

AI video generators can animate a character in seconds. With a few prompt tricks and the right export settings, that clip becomes a clean, looping sprite sheet your game engine can load.

1. Prompt for a sprite, not a film

Most AI video is made to look cinematic. For a sprite you want the opposite: a still camera and a simple background. Include phrases like these in your prompt:

  • "static camera, no camera movement, no zoom", so the character doesn't slide around the frame.
  • "full body, side view, centred" (or "front view") for a consistent angle.
  • "plain solid green background, evenly lit, no shadows" if you'll use the free green-screen removal.
  • "seamless loop" and a simple action: "walking in place", "idle breathing", "running cycle".

Generate a few versions and keep the one with the steadiest camera. A wobbling camera is the hardest problem to fix afterwards.

2. Choose a short section that loops

Load the clip in the converter and scrub through it. Set the start and end so they cover one full cycle: for a walk, from one foot touching down until the same foot touches down again. Short loops of 0.5 to 2 seconds make small, smooth sprite sheets.

3. Pick a game-friendly frame rate

Video is usually 24 to 30 frames a second, but games rarely need that many sprite frames. 8 to 12 FPS looks good for most characters and keeps the sheet small. Use 15 or more only for very fast or smooth motion.

4. Remove the background

  • Green or solid backdrop: tick Remove Background (Chroma Key), click the background in the preview to sample it, and adjust tolerance and spill. See removing a green screen cleanly.
  • Any other background: Pro adds AI background removal, which cuts the character out of a busy scene with no green screen. Choose Soft edges for fur and hair, or Crisp for cartoon and pixel styles.

5. Shrink it to sprite size

AI video is often 720p or 1080p, far bigger than a game sprite needs. Turn on Trim transparent padding to cut away the empty space, then lower Output Scale until the character is the size it will appear in your game. A character shown 128 pixels tall in-game doesn't need 1080-pixel frames.

6. Export and fix the loop

Tick Sprite sheet and Atlas metadata (.json), then process. If the loop has a small hitch, open the result in the Sprite Editor and delete the last frame if it repeats the first.

7. Load it in your engine

The JSON is in TexturePacker JSON Hash format, which most 2D engines understand. See the guides for Phaser and PixiJS and Godot.

Check the licence

Before shipping a commercial game, check the terms of the AI video service you used, so you know you're allowed to use its output in a product.
